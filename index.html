Xudong Tang

I am a third-year PhD candidate in Computer Science at Northwestern University, co-advised by Prof. Matt Groh and Prof. Jessica Hullman. My research is in human-AI collaboration, with a focus on how people perceive and interact with AI-generated content, especially synthetic voices and audio. Through large-scale behavioral experiments, I study which signals in audio shape human perception, and how synthetic content changes the relationship between those signals and what people perceive. In the social sciences more broadly, I also work on the validity of LLM simulations of human behavior in experiment and survey design. You can find me on LinkedIn and Google Scholar.

Previously, I spent many years doing research on graph neural networks for phylogenetics and Bayesian deep learning for microbiome data. Outside of my job, I am an enthusiastic part-time model maker, motorcyclist, and car and bicycle mechanic.

Ongoing projects

  • What makes a voice an identity signal? We ran a large-scale behavioral experiment on how people perceive identity similarity between voices, both real and synthetic. Our stimuli are recordings of English-speaking public figures from a wide range of linguistic backgrounds.

Human and Machine Perception of Voice Similarity in Authentic Speech and Voice Clones

Voice has been treated as a signature for identity. We can often recognize a speaker instantly and effortlessly in a crowded room or over a crackling phone line before understanding a single word. However, while human perception can uncover speaker identity reliably under favorable conditions like long, clear samples of familiar voices, it breaks down quickly under realistic degradations such as short samples, disguise, different languages, delays, and stress. These observations motivate an open question: What makes voice an identity signal?

We ran a large-scale online behavioral experiment to answer this question. We found that listeners often heard two recordings of the same person as two different people, and that they were more accurate when they were familiar with the speaker. Acoustically, two voices sounded less like the same person when they differed in average pitch, timbre, or the amount of noise in the voice. Listeners weighed these three differences more heavily than automatic speaker verification systems did.

The manuscript is in preparation. Stay tuned for the full results.
