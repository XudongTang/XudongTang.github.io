I am a third-year PhD candidate in Computer Science at Northwestern University, co-advised by Prof. Matt Groh and Prof. Jessica Hullman. My research is in human-AI collaboration, with a focus on how people perceive and interact with AI-generated content, especially synthetic voices and audio. Through large-scale behavioral experiments, I study which signals in audio shape human perception, and how synthetic content changes the relationship between those signals and what people perceive. In the social sciences more broadly, I also work on the validity of LLM simulations of human behavior in experiment and survey design. You can find me on LinkedIn and Google Scholar.

Human and Machine Perception of Voice Similarity in Authentic Speech and Voice Clones

Voice has been treated as a signature for identity. We can often recognize a speaker instantly and effortlessly in a crowded room or over a crackling phone line before understanding a single word. However, while human perception can uncover speaker identity reliably under favorable conditions like long, clear samples of familiar voices, it breaks down quickly under realistic degradations such as short samples, disguise, different languages, delays, and stress. These observations motivate an open question: What makes voice an identity signal?

We ran a large-scale online behavioral experiment to answer this question. We found that listeners often heard two recordings of the same person as two different people, and that they were more accurate when they were familiar with the speaker. Acoustically, two voices sounded less like the same person when they differed in average pitch, timbre, or the amount of noise in the voice. Listeners weighed these three differences more heavily than automatic speaker verification systems did.

VIPBench: A Human-Aligned Benchmark for Voice Identity Perception in the Age of Voice Cloning

Automatic speaker verification (ASV) systems have become highly accurate over the past decade and are now used in high-stakes settings like banking security. ASV systems are usually trained and tested on speaker labels from the recording metadata. But who actually produced a recording (its metadata identity) is not the same as who listeners think they are hearing (its perceived identity). For example, listeners sometimes hear two recordings of the same person as two different people. With generative AI, it's more important to measure perceived identity, because the metadata identity of a synthetic voice can be misleading or missing. A voice clone has the metadata identity of its source speaker, even when listeners hear the clone as someone else. A morph that mixes the voices of two speakers has no single metadata identity at all.

We introduce VIPBench, a voice identity benchmark built on human perception, and use it to test how well 11 popular speaker representation models align with human judgments. In the first two of our four tasks, we measure how well a model's similarity score predicts the benchmark target and the majority answer. In the other two, we measure whether a model and listeners find the same speaker pairs similar, and whether the relation between a model's scores and listener judgments is the same for real and synthetic recordings.

We found that listener judgments often differ from metadata identity. None of the 11 models is at the noise ceiling, which is the highest score a model can get given the random variation among listeners. Supervised speaker models align with listeners better than self-supervised models, and self-supervised models align best at their earliest layers. The three types of models are in the same order for accuracy on speaker labels and for alignment with listeners, but the order within each type differs. For example, resemblyzer has a 12% error rate on speaker labels, and it aligns with listeners as well as the four supervised models with error rates below 2%. With VIPBench, researchers can test speaker models against human perception on real, cloned, and morphed speech, and our results motivate training speaker models directly on listener judgments.
